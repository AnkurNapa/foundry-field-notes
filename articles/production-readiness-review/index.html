Production readiness review for a Foundry AI app

The review that decides whether an AI app on Microsoft Foundry goes live: which features are GA and which are still preview, the reference components and their jobs, the questions to put to the client, a checklist that maps to evidence and the anti-patterns that fail a review.

12 Oct 2026 Foundry as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Level 4 Ankur Napa GovernanceArchitectureEvaluation

Short answer

A readiness review answers one question with evidence: can this app take real traffic without surprising the people who own the risk? For Foundry that splits into five tests. Does every production dependency sit on a GA feature, or on a preview someone has approved in writing? Does every caller authenticate with Microsoft Entra ID and the narrowest role? Is there an evaluation result the business accepted, plus a gate that stops a worse version shipping? Can the team see a bad answer, roll back and pay the bill? Do the region, quota and network choices hold under the expected load?

The new Foundry portal is GA for core scenarios in Foundry projects. It is not GA everywhere. Monitoring, agent guardrails, memory, Voice Live and Operate are still preview. Workflows retire on 1 December 2026. A review that does not check the feature readiness table is not a review.

The decision

The first call is what the app is allowed to depend on. Each row is a trade-off the client must choose, not one you choose for them.

Questions to ask the client

  1. Which answers would cause harm if wrong? Returns and warranty answers cost money; a wrong delivery promise costs trust. Name them.
  2. Who owns the policy documents? How do they tell us when one changes?
  3. Is any preview feature acceptable in production? Who signs that off? For how long?
  4. What is the accepted quality bar, in numbers? On which test set?
  5. Who can approve a release? Who can roll one back at 8 PM on a Saturday?
  6. Which regions is data allowed to be processed in? Does Data Zone or Global processing pass that rule?
  7. What volume do you expect at peak? What latency will callers tolerate?
  8. Where do conversations get stored, for how long? Who can read them?
  9. What happens when the agent cannot answer? A person, a form, a phone number?
  10. What is the monthly budget? Who gets the alert when it is close?

Anti-patterns

  • Preview by accident. A dependency nobody noticed was preview, discovered when its API changes. The usual culprits are monitoring, memory and agent guardrails.
  • Building new work on Workflows. Foundry retires Workflows on 1 December 2026. Learn's direction is Microsoft Agent Framework for new development.
  • Owner as the developer role. Owner manages resources. It does not grant agent creation, yet it grants far more than a developer needs.
  • "It answered fine in the playground." One good conversation is a demo. A fixed test set with a threshold is evidence.
  • Deploy, then test, on an unpinned endpoint. Production changed before the first test ran.
  • Keys kept "just for the website". Every unlisted key is a credential nobody rotates and nobody can scope.
  • Classic agents in a new design. Classic agents are deprecated and retire on 31 March 2027. Do not start anything new on them.
  • No owner for the documents. A grounded agent is only as current as its files. Without an owner, it is confidently out of date by next quarter.

On the brewhouse floor

A food and drink plant never starts a new line without a pre-start review: allergens declared, labels checked against the recipe, a recall drill, someone with authority to stop the line. An AI app that answers customers about a product deserves the same. Plant B's product information agent would face the same checklist with one extra row: every allergen answer is in the fixed test set. It must pass at 100 per cent. Nobody gets to accept a lower number.

An AI landing zone: subscriptions, networks and identity for Foundry

A design guide for placing Foundry in an Azure landing zone: management groups for internal and internet-facing AI, one Foundry resource per production workload, hub-and-spoke networking with central DNS and Entra groups mapped to Foundry roles.

12 Oct 2026 Foundry as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Level 4 Ankur Napa ArchitectureNetworkingGovernance

Short answer

An Azure landing zone has two parts. The platform landing zone, usually one per Entra tenant, holds the management group hierarchy and shared services such as connectivity, DNS and security monitoring. Workload landing zones hold each workload's resources across its dev, test and production subscriptions. AI doesn't need a different architecture. Foundry resources go into workload landing zones like any other workload.

The Cloud Adoption Framework adds four AI-specific choices. Separate internet-facing and internal AI workloads into different management groups. Apply AI policies at those management groups. Deploy Foundry in workload subscriptions, not platform ones. In production, default to one Foundry resource per workload and treat sharing as a documented exception.

The decision

The biggest decision is how much to share. Learn's guidance gives opposite defaults for production and preproduction.

OptionGood forCostRisk
One Foundry resource per production workloadProduction. Learn's recommended defaultMore resources, networking and monitoring to runContained: a misconfiguration, outage or quota exhaustion hits one workload
One shared Foundry resource, one project per use caseExploration and dev/test. Learn's default for preproductionLowest. Reuses deployments, connections and network setupOne team's runaway evaluation can slow every other team
Shared production resource by exceptionWorkloads with the same data classification, network, identity boundary and ownerLower than isolationShared blast radius and quota pool. Splitting later is costly
Central Foundry resource per business group with workload projectsNot recommended by the Azure Architecture Center for landing zonesLooks cheapMuddles cost allocation and ownership

The second decision is the network model. The Foundry planning guide recommends a managed virtual network for a simpler secure setup and a bring-your-own virtual network for advanced control and custom routing. In a landing zone with a central firewall and DNS, bring your own is the usual fit, because the platform team already owns the hub.

Deployment order matters. Learn's landing zone architecture warns that the Agent Service deployment fails if its dependencies don't yet resolve to private endpoints from inside the spoke. Wait until DNS works, especially when DeployIfNotExists policies create the DNS records, before you deploy the agent capability.

Questions to ask the client

  1. Is there already a platform landing zone? Who runs the hub, the firewall and DNS?
  2. Which AI workloads face customers or the internet? Which are internal only?
  3. What sharing boundary fits the organisation: business unit, data domain or product owner?
  4. Which workloads handle regulated data? Do any need to process it in test?
  5. How are new subscriptions requested today? Is there a subscription vending process with a questionnaire?
  6. Which regions hold the data? Are the required models and agent features available there?
  7. Does any workload need two regions for availability? Is the extra cost justified?
  8. Which on-premises sources feed the agents? How much data moves? That decides VPN Gateway or ExpressRoute.
  9. Who owns cost, quota and incidents for each Foundry resource?

Review checklist

  • Internet-facing and internal AI workloads sit under different management groups.
  • Foundry resources live in workload subscriptions, never in the connectivity or identity subscriptions.
  • Each production workload has its own Foundry resource, or a written exception.
  • The sandbox has a quota ceiling and no production data.
  • All six Foundry and agent dependency DNS zones exist centrally and resolve from every spoke.
  • The agent subnet is delegated and sized before the Foundry resource is created. Outbound injection can't be added later.
  • Egress goes through the hub firewall with the agent identity endpoints allowed.
  • Developers reach Foundry through Bastion, VPN or ExpressRoute, not the public internet.
  • Entra groups, not people, hold Foundry roles at resource or project scope.
  • AI policies are assigned at the management groups, in Audit first.
  • Each Foundry resource has a named owner for cost, quota, access reviews and incidents.

Anti-patterns

  • A central AI subscription for everyone. Putting every team's Foundry work in a platform subscription makes the platform team a bottleneck and blurs cost. The Cloud Adoption Framework says to deploy AI resources in workload subscriptions.
  • Production colocation because it's cheaper. Learn says cost pressure alone doesn't justify sharing. The blast radius and quota pool become shared too.
  • A project per team as a security boundary for everything. Projects isolate agent capabilities well. Some older Foundry Tools APIs, such as speech-to-text, still need resource-scope access. Fine-tuning runs in the default project only. Check the capability mix before you rely on projects.
  • DNS left to each workload team. In a landing zone, the platform team usually owns the private DNS zones. Agree who creates the records before the first private endpoint.
  • Building outbound isolation later. Outbound VNet injection can't be added to an existing Foundry deployment. Retrofitting means a redeploy.
  • Skipping the second region question. Not every production workload needs two regions, but the decision should be on paper with the cost.

On the brewhouse floor

A brewing group with two breweries and a winery wants AI in three places: a public beer finder on its website, an internal quality assistant and a sandbox for the brewers' ideas. The beer finder goes under the online management group with its own subscription and Foundry resource. The quality assistant sits under corporate, in a spoke that reaches the plant historian over ExpressRoute and never faces the internet. The sandbox is shared, small and has no access to batch records, so the brewers can try things without risking the release data for FV-03.
